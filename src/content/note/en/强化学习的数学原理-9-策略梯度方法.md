**Question for this chapter:** can we optimize the policy parameters directly, without repeatedly taking a greedy action over learned values? [Chapter 8](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-8-价值函数近似) parameterized values. This chapter parameterizes action probabilities and derives the gradient of a precisely stated performance objective.

[Series guide](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-0-阅读指南) · [中文摘要](/note/强化学习的数学原理-9-策略梯度方法)

We need the Bellman equation, differentiation, conditional expectation, and the distinction between a probability distribution and an unnormalized weighting. We will derive the discounted policy gradient, connect it to trajectory sampling, and check both its direction and its variance numerically.

## A differentiable stochastic policy

For finite actions, assign preferences $h_\theta(s,a)$ and normalize them:

$$
\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\frac{\exp h_\theta(s,a)}{\sum_b\exp h_\theta(s,b)}.
$$

The log derivative is

$$
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)
=\nabla_\theta h_\theta(s,a)
-\sum_b\pi_\theta(b\mid s)\nabla_\theta h_\theta(s,b).
$$

This follows by differentiating $h_\theta(s,a)-\log\sum_b e^{h_\theta(s,b)}$. For tabular preferences, it becomes “one-hot chosen action minus action probabilities.” Increasing the chosen preference alone also changes the probabilities of every other action through normalization.

For our two-action grid, use one logit per nonterminal state:

$$
p_s=\pi_\theta(R\mid s)=\sigma(\theta_s),\qquad
\pi_\theta(D\mid s)=1-p_s.
$$

Then the only nonzero score component at state $s$ is $\mathbf1\{a=R\}-p_s$. The policy at $T$ is fixed and independent of $\theta$, since no further reward is possible. Initial logits zero give the uniform policy. Finite logits give positive action probabilities, but they do not alone guarantee useful exploration in a large environment.

## Choose the objective before taking its gradient

Our main objective uses a **fixed initial distribution** $\rho$:

$$
J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{S_0\sim\rho,\pi_\theta}
\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^tR_{t+1}\right]
=\rho^Tv_{\pi_\theta}.
$$

In the grid, $\rho$ puts probability one on $A$, so $J=v_\pi(A)$. Rewards and transition probabilities depend on state and action but not directly on $\theta$. We assume differentiable policies and sufficient regularity to interchange expectations, sums, and derivatives; bounded rewards, discounting, and locally bounded policy derivatives suffice in our finite-state setting.

Other objectives require other weightings:

| Objective | State weighting and caveat |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed-start discounted return $\rho^Tv_\pi$ | Discounted occupancy from $\rho$; our experiments use this |
| Stationary average reward $\bar r_\pi=d_\pi^Tr_\pi$ | Requires a suitable continuing chain; the differential action value appears in its gradient |
| Stationary average discounted value $d_\pi^Tv_\pi$ | Both $d_\pi$ and $v_\pi$ depend on the policy |

For a stationary $d_\pi$, multiplying the discounted Bellman equation by $d_\pi^T$ gives

$$
(1-\gamma)d_\pi^Tv_\pi=d_\pi^Tr_\pi.
$$

Thus the last two objectives are proportional for fixed $\gamma$, but their derivatives must account for the policy dependence of $d_\pi$. This identity does not turn a fixed-start objective into a stationary one. In the absorbing grid, stationary average reward is zero for every policy that reaches $T$ almost surely, making it unsuitable for measuring how quickly reward is reached.

## Derive the discounted policy gradient

For compactness write $v=v_{\pi_\theta}$ and $q=q_{\pi_\theta}$. Differentiate

$$
v(s)=\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q(s,a).
$$

The product rule gives

$$
\nabla v(s)=\sum_a\nabla\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q(s,a)
+\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\nabla q(s,a).
$$

Because the environment does not directly depend on the parameters,

$$
\nabla q(s,a)=\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\nabla v(s').
$$

Define the local policy derivative $g(s)=\sum_a\nabla\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q(s,a)$. For each parameter coordinate, the resulting vector equation is

$$
\nabla v=g+\gamma P_\pi\nabla v,
\qquad \nabla v=(I-\gamma P_\pi)^{-1}g.
$$

The inverse exists by Chapter 2. Define the **unnormalized discounted occupancy**

$$
m_{\rho,\pi}^T=\rho^T(I-\gamma P_\pi)^{-1}
=\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\rho^TP_\pi^t.
$$

Its component $m(s)$ is the discounted expected number of visits to state $s$. If the full absorbing chain is retained, $\sum_s m(s)=1/(1-\gamma)$. It is not a probability distribution. The normalized distribution is $d_\gamma(s)=(1-\gamma)m(s)$.

Multiplying by $\rho^T$ and using $\nabla\pi=\pi\nabla\log\pi$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla J(\theta)
&=\sum_s m(s)\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)
\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)q(s,a)\\
&=\frac1{1-\gamma}
\mathbb E_{S\sim d_\gamma,A\sim\pi_\theta}
[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(A\mid S)q(S,A)].
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the discounted policy gradient theorem for the objective we chose. The derivation does not ignore how future state visitation depends on the policy; the Bellman resolvent has already accounted for that dependence. The normalized form needs the factor $1/(1-\gamma)$. One can absorb a constant into a learning rate, but an equality of mathematical gradients still needs the constant.

## From the theorem to REINFORCE

Since $m(s)=\sum_t\gamma^t\Pr(S_t=s)$, the theorem can be written

$$
\nabla J
=\mathbb E_\pi\left[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t
\nabla\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)q_\pi(S_t,A_t)\right].
$$

A full suffix return satisfies $\mathbb E[G_t\mid S_t,A_t]=q_\pi(S_t,A_t)$. Replacing $q_\pi$ with $G_t$ therefore yields the unbiased episodic estimator

$$
\widehat g=\sum_{t=0}^{\tau_{\mathrm{end}}-1}
\gamma^t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)G_t.
$$

The two discount factors have different jobs: $G_t$ discounts rewards relative to time $t$, while $\gamma^t$ weights when the policy decision occurs relative to the episode's start. Dropping the outer factor generally changes the estimator for our fixed-start discounted objective.

A second derivation starts with a trajectory probability

$$
p_\theta(\tau)=\rho(S_0)\prod_t
\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)p(S_{t+1},R_{t+1}\mid S_t,A_t).
$$

Its log derivative is the sum of policy scores; environment terms differentiate to zero. Applying $\nabla p=p\nabla\log p$ initially gives $\mathbb E[G_0\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_t]$. Rewards received before action $A_t$ can be removed from its score term because that score has conditional mean zero given the past. The remaining rewards equal $\gamma^tG_t$, yielding the same estimator. This causality argument explains why using reward-to-go avoids irrelevant past-reward noise.

A direct implementation is:

```text
freeze theta for one complete episode
sample the episode using pi_theta
compute all suffix returns backward
g = sum_t gamma^t * score_theta(s_t, a_t) * G_t
perform one ascent update: theta += actor_step * g
```

Freezing the policy keeps all actions and scores associated with the same parameter value. Recomputing later score terms after an in-place actor update would need a different analysis. Our experiment accumulates first and updates once per episode.

## Baselines preserve the mean, not every variance

At each fixed state,

$$
\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)
=\nabla\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=0.
$$

Therefore subtracting an action-independent baseline $b(s)$ changes no expected gradient:

$$
\widehat g_b=\sum_t\gamma^t
\nabla\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)[G_t-b(S_t)].
$$

A baseline may depend on $\theta$, time, state, or preceding training data, provided it is fixed with respect to the current action when conditioning on the information before that action. During the actor update, stop gradients through the baseline. Its role is a control variate, not an extra reward term to differentiate.

A baseline fitted on the very same action/return being multiplied by the score can introduce dependence and bias. The training experiment uses a copy of value estimates fitted on preceding episodes; it updates the baseline only after forming those episode terms. The variance experiment uses the exact value from the model solely as a controlled reference.

A common baseline is $v_\pi(s)$. It centers the return around state value, but it is not always variance-optimal. For a single score term $z(G-b)$ at a fixed state, minimizing its trace covariance is equivalent to minimizing $\mathbb E[\|z\|^2(G-b)^2\mid s]$. Differentiation gives

$$
b^*(s)=\frac{\mathbb E[\|z\|^2G\mid s]}{\mathbb E[\|z\|^2\mid s]},
$$

when the denominator is positive. The score-norm weighting can make this different from $v_\pi(s)$. For a whole trajectory's sum, correlations between time steps also matter. An arbitrarily chosen baseline can increase variance.

### A hand-checkable gradient

At uniform logits in the grid, the gradient is

$$
\nabla_\theta J=(0,-9/242,9/242)^T
\approx(0,-0.037190,0.037190)^T.
$$

The $A$ component is zero by symmetry. At $B$, increasing the right-action probability increases time spent at the boundary, so the derivative is negative. At $C$, increasing the right-action probability speeds entry to $T$, so it is positive.

For example, $q(B,R)=9/11$, $q(B,D)=1$, and the local derivative is $(1/4)(9/11-1)=-1/22$. Discounted occupancy of $B$ from $A$ is $0.45/(1-0.45)=9/11$. Their product is $-9/242$. This verifies both the sign and the visitation factor.

## Where the average-reward formula differs

For a smooth family of finite ergodic continuing chains, define $\eta(\theta)=d_\pi^Tr_\pi$. A differential value $h$ satisfies the Poisson equation

$$
\eta\mathbf1+h=r_\pi+P_\pi h.
$$

Its additive constant is arbitrary. Define $q^{\mathrm{diff}}(s,a)=\bar r(s,a)-\eta+\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)h(s')$. Differentiating the Poisson equation gives, coordinate by coordinate,

$$
\nabla\eta\mathbf1+\nabla h
=u+P_\pi\nabla h,
\qquad u(s)=\sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s)q^{\mathrm{diff}}(s,a).
$$

Multiply by $d_\pi^T$ and use stationarity to cancel the $\nabla h$ terms:

$$
\nabla\eta=\sum_s d_\pi(s)\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)
\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s)q^{\mathrm{diff}}(s,a).
$$

The stationary distribution is appropriate here, and the value is differential rather than the discounted $q$ above. Changing an objective or dropping discounting requires revisiting both definitions. This section explains the other main metric in the source; our numerical experiments remain fixed-start discounted tasks.

## Reproduce gradient and variance checks

The [script](/rl/ch7-10.py) solves $v_\pi$ exactly and compares the analytic gradient with central finite differences at three logit vectors. With difference step $10^{-5}$, the largest observed coordinate discrepancy is below $9\times10^{-12}$; the check tolerance is $10^{-9}$. This verifies the occupancy factor and the chosen objective, independently of sampled learning curves.

For variance, freeze $\theta=(-0.4,0.7,-0.9)$ and use 100 independent seeds (300–399), each averaging 100 episodes. Both baseline variants receive the same episodes within each seed. The exact gradient is approximately $(0.006441,-0.050393,0.085369)$.

| Estimator | Trace of covariance of batch gradients | Component RMSE to exact gradient |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No baseline | 0.003010 | 0.031636 |
| Exact-value baseline | 0.000295 | 0.009913 |

The covariance uses the sample denominator 99. It describes the distribution of 100-episode batch estimates, not of individual transitions. The baseline reduces measured variance in this experiment; sampled means need not equal the exact gradient to all printed digits. The [JSON](/rl/results-7-10.json) records those means as well.

A separate learning run uses REINFORCE with the preceding-episode learned baseline, not an oracle baseline. Over 20 seeds and 3,000 episodes, its final mean exact policy value from $A$ is about $0.893609$, with sample standard deviation $0.000401$. The optimum is $0.9$. Finite softmax logits retain some suboptimal action probability; a successful finite run need not exactly attain the deterministic optimum.

There is no general claim of global optimality for arbitrary neural policy parameterizations. The theorem identifies a gradient; convergence of a particular optimizer requires its own assumptions.

## Check your understanding

1. Is $m(s)$ a probability distribution? **No.** In the full discounted chain its total mass is $1/(1-\gamma)$.
2. Why does the policy gradient theorem not contain an explicit derivative of occupancy? **The Bellman recursion has already accounted for future policy effects.** It was not treated as constant during derivation.
3. May we omit $\gamma^t$ when summing every decision in an episode? **Not for the stated fixed-start discounted objective without changing the sampling convention or estimator.**
4. Does every baseline reduce variance? **No.** It preserves the mean under the stated conditions; its quality determines the variance.
5. Why is the stationary average reward unhelpful in this absorbing grid? **After absorption every reward is zero**, although discounted arrival returns differ.
