**Question for this chapter:** can we learn from one transition before the complete return becomes available? [Chapter 5](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-5-蒙特卡洛方法) waited for an episode; [Chapter 6](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-6-随机近似) explained noisy incremental updates. Temporal-difference learning combines a sampled reward with an estimated continuation value.

[Series guide](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-0-阅读指南) · [中文摘要](/note/强化学习的数学原理-7-时序差分方法)

By the end, we should be able to derive each target, distinguish behavior and target policies, and implement terminal handling and delayed n-step updates. We keep the finite discounted MDP and the same four-state Gridworld. Values at terminal state $T$ remain zero.

## TD(0): a sampled Bellman update

For a fixed stationary policy $\pi$, the Bellman equation is

$$
v_\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[R_{t+1}+\gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1})\mid S_t=s].
$$

Write the vector root equation as $F(V)=V-T_\pi V=0$. Given the past $\mathcal F_t$ and current state $S_t$, a transition sampled under $\pi$ supplies the target

$$
Y_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V_t(S_{t+1}),\qquad
\delta_t=Y_t-V_t(S_t).
$$

Update only the visited component:

$$
V_{t+1}(S_t)=V_t(S_t)+\alpha_t(S_t)\delta_t.
$$

This is **bootstrapping**: the target uses an estimate, rather than the entire remaining sampled return. Our sign convention is **target minus estimate**, with an additive update. The source chapter sometimes uses the opposite TD-error sign together with subtraction; the algorithms agree when both signs are changed consistently.

Since $V_t$ is known given the past,

$$
\mathbb E[\delta_t\mid\mathcal F_t,S_t=s]
=(T_\pi V_t)(s)-V_t(s).
$$

Thus the mean update follows the Bellman residual. At $V_t=v_\pi$, the conditional mean error is zero; the error of an individual transition need not vanish. A target based on an inaccurate $V_t$ is generally biased as an estimate of $v_\pi(s)$. That does not prevent convergence of the coupled root-finding process.

### One transition by hand

Suppose $V(A)=0.4$, $V(B)=0.6$, $\alpha=0.1$, and the observed transition is $A\xrightarrow{R,0}B$. Then

$$
Y=0+0.9(0.6)=0.54,\quad \delta=0.14,\quad V_{\mathrm{new}}(A)=0.414.
$$

If the next transition is $B\xrightarrow{D,1}T$, its target is exactly 1, regardless of any uninitialized terminal entry. With $V(B)=0.6$, that update gives $V_{\mathrm{new}}(B)=0.64$.

Only the second update directly observes the reward. Later visits propagate its information backward. MC would instead wait for the full path and use returns $0.9$ and $1$ for the two states.

### What supports convergence?

A standard sufficient tabular setting is: finite states, $0\le\gamma<1$, bounded rewards, a fixed policy, correct conditional transition samples, and infinitely many updates to every state whose value is to be learned. Choose the step size from information available before observing that transition's reward and next state, rather than adapting it to the resulting noise. Along each state's own visit sequence, require

$$
\sum_n\alpha_n(s)=\infty,\qquad
\sum_n\alpha_n(s)^2<\infty,\qquad 0<\alpha_n(s)\le1.
$$

Under these conditions tabular TD converges almost surely to $v_\pi$. Restarted episodes may provide the required visits to transient nonterminal states. An absorbing terminal state need not be sampled forever when its known value is held at zero.

The proof uses asynchronous stochastic approximation for a contraction, rather than the scalar independent-mean result alone. Let $e_t=V_t-v_\pi$. The error in the sampled target can be split into

$$
Y_t-v_\pi(S_t)
=\gamma(P_\pi e_t)(S_t)+M_{t+1},
$$

where $\mathbb E[M_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t,S_t]=0$. The drift satisfies $\|\gamma P_\pi e_t\|_\infty\le\gamma\|e_t\|_\infty$. Bounded rewards and convex updates keep the iterates bounded: a bound $\max(\|V_0\|_\infty,R_{\max}/(1-\gamma))$ is preserved. Consequently the conditional noise variance is bounded. These are the drift, noise, and visitation ingredients of the asynchronous contraction theorem used in the source's convergence analysis. This is a proof outline identifying the external theorem, not a claim to prove that theorem from scratch.

A constant step size does not meet the square-summability condition. It can track changing values, but sample paths generally keep fluctuating. Likewise, changing the policy changes the root; fixed-policy prediction theory cannot by itself justify a control algorithm.

## From state values to Sarsa

Without a model, $V$ alone does not tell us which action is better. Learn $Q(s,a)$ instead. After observing $(S_t,A_t,R_{t+1},S_{t+1})$, select the next actual action $A_{t+1}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S_{t+1})$ and form

$$
Y_t^{\mathrm{Sarsa}}=R_{t+1}+\gamma Q_t(S_{t+1},A_{t+1}).
$$

Then

$$
Q_{t+1}(S_t,A_t)=Q_t(S_t,A_t)
+\alpha_t(S_t,A_t)[Y_t^{\mathrm{Sarsa}}-Q_t(S_t,A_t)].
$$

The name records the five quantities **state, action, reward, state, action**. The next action used in the target must be carried into the next environment step, rather than resampled independently after the update. At termination, skip the next action and use the reward alone.

For control, choose actions with an $\epsilon$-greedy policy derived from $Q$, then repeat evaluation and improvement incrementally. Sarsa is on-policy when this same exploratory policy supplies both actual and target actions. With fixed $\epsilon>0$, it evaluates exploratory behavior, not a fully greedy policy. Tabular optimal-control convergence requires additional conditions such as GLIE—every pair explored infinitely often while the behavior becomes greedy in the limit—and per-pair step-size conditions. Merely decreasing $\epsilon$ does not prove infinite exploration.

## Expected Sarsa: integrate out one random variable

Instead of sampling the next action in the target, average over the target policy:

$$
Y_t^{\mathrm{ES}}=R_{t+1}
+\gamma\sum_a\pi_t(a\mid S_{t+1})Q_t(S_{t+1},a).
$$

This requires knowledge of our policy probabilities, not the environment's transition probabilities. Conditional on $(S_t,A_t,R_{t+1},S_{t+1})$ and the current estimates, it is the conditional expectation of the Sarsa target. The law of total variance therefore removes the next-action sampling variance, holding the policy and $Q_t$ fixed. It does not prove that every Expected Sarsa learning curve has lower error.

For a two-action row $Q(B,R)=0.8$, $Q(B,D)=1.0$, and $\epsilon=0.2$, the action probabilities are $(0.1,0.9)$. After $A\to B$ with reward zero, the Expected Sarsa target is

$$
0.9[0.1(0.8)+0.9(1.0)]=0.882.
$$

Sarsa samples either $0.72$ or $0.9$; their mean is $0.882$. A greedy target would be $0.9$. These targets solve different evaluation problems.

## n-step Sarsa: delay the bootstrap

Let $\tau_{\mathrm{end}}$ be the episode's terminal time and let $h=\min(t+n,\tau_{\mathrm{end}})$. The target for $(S_t,A_t)$ is

$$
G_{t:t+n}=\sum_{k=t}^{h-1}\gamma^{k-t}R_{k+1}
+\mathbf 1\{t+n<\tau_{\mathrm{end}}\}\gamma^n Q(S_{t+n},A_{t+n}).
$$

Here $Q$ in the bootstrap is the estimate available when that delayed update occurs. For $n=1$ this is Sarsa. If the horizon reaches termination, there is no bootstrap and the target is a sampled complete suffix return.

Implementation needs a buffer of states, actions, and rewards. At environment time $t$, update index $u=t-n+1$ if it is nonnegative. When the environment terminates, keep flushing pending indices through $\tau_{\mathrm{end}}-1$; do not stop the update loop immediately. Never read $A_{\tau_{\mathrm{end}}}$.

On $A\to B\to T$, two-step Sarsa assigns target $0+0.9(1)=0.9$ to the first pair. With $Q(A,R)=0.2$ and $\alpha=0.5$, the result is $0.55$. Larger $n$ uses more actual rewards and less bootstrapping, but also waits longer and incorporates more trajectory randomness. There is no universally best $n$.

A time limit is different from a true terminal state. For a continuing task cut into chunks, the last observed state still has future value and normally needs a bootstrap. The script below aborts an unexpectedly long episode instead of silently treating a limit as termination.

## Q-learning: an optimality target

The optimal action-value equation replaces the next-policy average with a maximum. Its sampled target is

$$
Y_t^{Q}=R_{t+1}+\gamma\max_a Q_t(S_{t+1},a).
$$

The behavior policy $\beta$ chooses $A_t$, while the target follows a greedy continuation. These policies can differ, so this is off-policy learning. No action importance ratio is needed for this tabular one-step target: conditional on the sampled pair $(s,a)$, the environment transition law is the required one, and the maximization supplies the target continuation directly.

For finite discounted MDPs with bounded rewards, every pair updated infinitely often, and the per-pair step-size conditions above, tabular Q-learning converges almost surely to $q^*$. The key contraction is

$$
\|\mathcal T_*Q-\mathcal T_*\widetilde Q\|_\infty
\le\gamma\|Q-\widetilde Q\|_\infty.
$$

It follows from the same maximum inequality used in Chapter 3. Conditional mean drift equals $\mathcal T_*Q-Q$; bounded iterates and noise allow the asynchronous contraction result to apply. This theorem does not carry over automatically to a shared neural network.

The maximum of noisy estimates can be upward biased. Expected Sarsa removes action-sampling noise; Q-learning's maximization is a different operation and does not provide that variance guarantee.

## One table connecting DP, MC, and TD

All rows use an update toward the listed target. The DP row evaluates a fixed policy.

| Method | Target | Model and sampling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DP evaluation | $(T_\pi V)(s)$ | Model expectation over all next outcomes |
| MC prediction | $G_t$ | Full sampled episode, no bootstrap |
| TD(0) | $R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})$ | One transition, bootstrap |
| Sarsa | $R_{t+1}+\gamma Q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})$ | Sampled target-policy action |
| Expected Sarsa | $R_{t+1}+\gamma\sum_a\pi(a\mid S_{t+1})Q(S_{t+1},a)$ | Average target-policy actions |
| n-step Sarsa | $G_{t:t+n}$ | Up to $n$ rewards and one bootstrap |
| Q-learning | $R_{t+1}+\gamma\max_aQ(S_{t+1},a)$ | Greedy target, exploratory behavior allowed |

All terminal bootstrap terms are zero. “Model-free” does not mean “assumption-free”: the meaning of the sampled transition and the coverage of relevant pairs still matter.

## Check your understanding

1. Can a correct value function have a nonzero sampled TD error? **Yes.** Only its conditional mean vanishes under the evaluated policy.
2. Does Expected Sarsa require the environment model? **No.** It averages known policy probabilities over actions at an observed next state.
3. Why must n-step Sarsa flush its buffer after termination? **The final state-action pairs have pending shorter returns.** Dropping them loses valid training samples.
4. Can Q-learning learn greedy values while behaving randomly? **Yes, with adequate coverage and the tabular convergence assumptions.** The sampled current action and the target's next action have different roles.
5. Is fixed-$\epsilon$ Sarsa guaranteed to learn $q^*$? **No.** It retains exploratory actions in its target policy.
