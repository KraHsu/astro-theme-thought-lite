**Question for this chapter:** if the transition and reward models are unavailable, can we still evaluate and improve a policy? The definition $q_\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a]$ suggests a direct approach: observe returns and average them.

Read [Chapter 4](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-4-价值迭代与策略迭代) first for policy evaluation and improvement. We keep the same Gridworld, reward timing, and $\gamma=0.9$.

## Estimating an expectation with samples

If $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are independent samples from the same distribution with finite variance, then the sample mean

$$
\hat\mu_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i
$$

has expectation $\mu=\mathbb E[X]$ and variance $\sigma^2/n$. The law of large numbers describes its convergence to $\mu$ as the sample count grows. A single realization need not get closer to $\mu$ at every update.

For policy evaluation, the samples are returns generated under a **fixed policy**. Their uncertainty comes from action selection, environment transitions, rewards, or initial conditions. Merely calling a dataset “experience” does not guarantee independent samples or the correct policy distribution.

For independent episodes starting at $(s,a)$, take $a$ once and follow $\pi$ afterward. Then

$$
\hat q_n(s,a)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nG_0^{(i)}\longrightarrow q_\pi(s,a).
$$

This update does not use $p(s'\mid s,a)$ or $\bar r(s,a)$. A simulator still contains environment dynamics internally; “model-free” describes the information consumed by the learning update, not the absence of an environment.

## Computing returns and reusing an episode

Consider the same episode as in Chapter 1:

$$
A\xrightarrow[0]{R}B\xrightarrow[0]{R}B\xrightarrow[1]{D}T.
$$

Process rewards backward with $G_T=0$ and $G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}$. The three returns are

$$
G_2=1,\qquad G_1=0.9,\qquad G_0=0.81.
$$

One episode provides several state or state-action observations. We must specify which repeated visits contribute:

- **First-visit MC:** use only the earliest occurrence of each state, or each state-action pair, in an episode.
- **Every-visit MC:** use each occurrence. These samples are correlated within an episode, so the simple independent-sample variance formula cannot be applied blindly.

For first-visit state prediction, this episode contributes $0.81$ to $A$ and $0.9$ to $B$. Every-visit prediction contributes both $0.9$ and $1$ to $B$. For first-visit action prediction, $(B,R)$ and $(B,D)$ are different pairs and contribute $0.9$ and $1$, respectively.

A common implementation bug is to compute returns backward and treat the first pair encountered during that backward scan as the first visit. That actually selects the **last** visit in chronological order. Our script computes the return array backward, then scans the episode forward to apply first-visit updates.

### Incremental averaging

We do not need to store every return. If $N(s,a)$ counts samples for a pair, update

$$
\begin{aligned}
N(s,a)&\leftarrow N(s,a)+1,\\
Q(s,a)&\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\frac{G_t-Q(s,a)}{N(s,a)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This follows by separating the latest sample from the old sample sum. The denominator is the visit count for this pair, not the global episode count. Replacing $1/N$ by a general step size leads to Chapter 6.

## MC Basic: sampled policy iteration

The simplest control method replaces the exact evaluation step of policy iteration with sampled action values:

```text
initialize pi
repeat:
    freeze pi for this entire evaluation batch
    for each nonterminal state s and action a:
        generate many episodes starting at (s, a), then following pi
        Q(s, a) ← average initial return of those episodes
    pi ← greedy policy with respect to Q
```

Why estimate q rather than v? Greedy improvement can select an action directly from q. If only v were estimated, obtaining action values for improvement would still require the environment model.

The price is expensive sampling and the ability to reset the environment to arbitrary starting pairs. Changing the policy halfway through a batch would also change the distribution being estimated, so the frozen-policy step is deliberate.

Exact q values would recover the policy-improvement theorem. With finite noisy estimates, a greedy update can choose the wrong action. If each action-value error is at most $\delta$ and the true best action has a gap greater than $2\delta$, its ranking is preserved. A fixed finite sample budget alone is not a general convergence theorem.

## Exploring starts: use more of each episode

MC Exploring Starts makes two changes: reuse returns from visited pairs, and improve the policy after each episode rather than after a full evaluation of every pair.

```text
initialize Q, per-pair counts, and pi
repeat:
    sample an initial (s, a) with positive probability for every pair
    generate an episode: force a first, then follow the current pi
    compute all returns backward
    scan forward; update each pair on its first visit using its own count
    pi ← greedy(Q)
```

Exploring starts is a coverage assumption. A deterministic greedy policy may otherwise never try an action whose initial estimate is too low. Randomizing the first pair permits continued sampling of all pairs even while the policy after the first action is greedy.

This assumption may be easy in a simulator and impossible on a physical robot. It also does not guarantee termination: a greedy policy can loop. The finite-horizon handling used in our experiment is explained below rather than hidden in the algorithm.

## Epsilon-greedy control without arbitrary starting pairs

Let $m=|\mathcal A(s)|$, and choose one greedy action $a_g(s)$. An epsilon-greedy policy assigns

$$
\pi_\epsilon(a\mid s)
=(1-\epsilon)\mathbf1\{a=a_g(s)\}+\frac{\epsilon}{m},
\qquad 0<\epsilon\le1.
$$

For two actions and $\epsilon=0.2$, the greedy action has probability $0.9$, not $0.8$: it also receives its share of random exploration.

```text
initialize Q and an epsilon-soft policy
repeat:
    start an episode from the allowed initial-state distribution
    follow the current epsilon-greedy policy until the episode ends
    compute returns; update first-visit pair averages
    pi ← epsilon-greedy(Q)
```

A fixed positive epsilon keeps exploration alive. Among policies satisfying $\pi(a\mid s)\ge\epsilon/m$, putting the remaining mass on a maximizing q action maximizes the expected q. Hence an exact epsilon-greedy improvement improves an already epsilon-soft policy within that constrained class. The sample-based version still has estimation error.

Two distinctions matter:

- **Action coverage is not state coverage.** Positive action probabilities help only at states that can actually be reached from the initial distribution.
- **A fixed-epsilon behavior policy is not generally an unrestricted optimum.** It keeps taking suboptimal actions. To seek a fully greedy limit while retaining infinite exploration, one needs suitable GLIE conditions—greedy in the limit with infinite exploration—and the other convergence assumptions. Simply writing $\epsilon_n\to0$ does not establish those conditions.

Here the same epsilon-greedy policy both generates the episodes and is evaluated, so this is on-policy learning. A separate greedy policy can be extracted for evaluation, but its value is not identical to the value of the exploratory behavior policy.

## Episodes and time limits

Classical episodic MC waits for completed returns. Discounting makes infinite returns finite but does not make an infinite episode finish.

Our script caps simulated rollouts at $H=200$ transitions and gives a nonterminal endpoint a zero tail estimate. If rewards are bounded by $R_{\max}$, the error in the initial return is bounded by

$$
|G_0-G_0^{(H)}|\le\frac{\gamma^HR_{\max}}{1-\gamma}.
$$

For a reused return at time $t$, the bound is $\gamma^{H-t}R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$, not $\gamma^H R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$. In this particular Gridworld, first visits to nonterminal states occur at time 0 or 1. First visits to state-action pairs can occur later: for example, the exit action at $B$ may follow many loops. That exit terminates immediately, so its observed suffix return is complete. In a truncated episode, the observed nonterminal pairs are only the starting move and a boundary-loop action, first visited at time 0 or 1. Thus the worst missing-tail bound for the first-visit updates actually used here is $0.9^{199}/0.1<7.84\times10^{-9}$. This is a controlled approximation, not an assertion that truncation and termination are equivalent.

The script retains truncated samples and records truncation counts; discarding unsuccessful episodes could bias the dataset. Fixed-policy prediction uses the uniform policy, which terminates almost surely here, and checks that none of its sampled episodes hit the cap. Some early exploring-starts control episodes do hit it because the current greedy policy loops.

## Experiment: compare samples with the known answer

Run the [shared script](/rl/ch1-6.py); the recorded [results JSON](/rl/results.json) includes parameters and seeds. The learning updates use only episode samples. The exact model from Chapter 2 is used separately as an evaluation oracle.

For first-visit prediction of the uniform policy, each episode starts at $A$. Across seeds 0–9, the mean root-mean-square error over the three nonterminal states is:

| Episodes per seed | Mean state RMSE | Standard deviation across seeds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 0.012451 | 0.006801 |
| 1,000 | 0.003703 | 0.001901 |
| 10,000 | 0.001293 | 0.000527 |

The exact vector is $(9/11,10/11,10/11,0)$. These standard deviations describe variation across ten runs; they are not confidence intervals or a convergence proof.

For control, seeds 0–4 give:

| Method | Sampling budget per seed | Extracted greedy value at $A$ | Behavior value at $A$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MC Basic | 6 rounds, 300 rollouts per nonterminal pair per round | 0.9 | 0.9 |
| MC Exploring Starts | 10,000 episodes | 0.9 | 0.9 |
| MC epsilon-greedy, epsilon 0.2 | 10,000 episodes | 0.9 | 0.890110 |

All five runs of each method recover a greedy policy with zero value gap against the exact optimum on all states. This easy example illustrates the algorithms; it is not evidence of universal reliability or a fair sample-efficiency ranking, since rollout lengths and sampling budgets differ.

The epsilon-greedy behavior value can also be checked analytically. At $B$, it exits with probability $0.9$ and loops with probability $0.1$, so $v_B=0.9+0.09v_B=90/91$. By symmetry $v_C=90/91$, and $v_A=0.9(90/91)=81/91\approx0.890110$.

## Check your understanding

1. What does MC need instead of a known probability model? **Sampled trajectories** generated under the policy being evaluated.
2. Why can greedy-only learning fail? **Unsampled actions may remain underestimated**, preventing further exploration.
3. Does epsilon 0.2 mean the nongreedy action has probability 0.2 in our two-action grid? **No:** its probability is 0.1.
4. Can returns collected under changing policies be treated as i.i.d. estimates of one fixed q function? **No:** this is part of the additional difficulty of control compared with fixed-policy prediction.
