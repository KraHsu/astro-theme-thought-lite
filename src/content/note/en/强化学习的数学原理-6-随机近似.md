**Question for this chapter:** why can an update using one noisy observation at a time approach the solution of an expectation equation? [Chapter 5](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-5-蒙特卡洛方法) already used such an update for Monte Carlo averages. Here we derive it, generalize it to root finding and optimization, and state sufficient convergence assumptions.

[Series guide](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-0-阅读指南) · [中文摘要](/note/强化学习的数学原理-6-随机近似)

## Incremental means are the first example

For observations $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, let $w_n$ be their sample mean. Algebra gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_n&=\frac{(n-1)w_{n-1}+X_n}{n}\\
&=w_{n-1}+\frac1n(X_n-w_{n-1}).
\end{aligned}
$$

Only the old estimate, the new observation, and the count are needed. Generalize the step size to $\alpha_n$:

$$
w_n=w_{n-1}+\alpha_n(X_n-w_{n-1}).
$$

For independent identically distributed observations with mean $\mu$, write $X_n=\mu+\xi_n$ and $e_n=w_n-\mu$. Then

$$
e_n=(1-\alpha_n)e_{n-1}+\alpha_n\xi_n.
$$

The first term contracts the old error; the second injects fresh noise. Understanding their competition is the central problem of stochastic approximation.

As a hand calculation, take returns $0.9,0.81,0.729$. With $w_0=0$ and $\alpha_n=1/n$, the estimates are $0.9$, $0.855$, and $0.813$. The third value is exactly $(0.9+0.81+0.729)/3$.

## Robbins–Monro: solving an equation with noisy observations

Suppose the goal is to solve $g(w^*)=0$, but evaluating $g$ exactly is impossible. Instead, an observation at the current iterate is

$$
Y_n=g(w_n)+\eta_{n+1}.
$$

The Robbins–Monro update is

$$
w_{n+1}=w_n-\alpha_nY_n.
$$

For an increasing $g$, the minus sign moves a positive residual to the left and a negative residual to the right. A wrong sign makes the root repelling: with $g(w)=w-1$ and a positive constant step, adding $\alpha g(w)$ multiplies the error by $1+\alpha$.

Mean estimation is a special case. Set $g(w)=w-\mu$ and observe $Y_n=w_n-X_{n+1}$. Then $\eta_{n+1}=\mu-X_{n+1}$, and RM becomes the incremental-mean update.

### Sufficient assumptions, not just a step-size recipe

Here is a convenient scalar version. Let $\mathcal F_n$ contain all information available before observing $\eta_{n+1}$. Assume:

1. $w_0$ has finite second moment; $g$ is continuously differentiable on $\mathbb R$, with $0<m\le g'(w)\le M<\infty$ for every $w$.
2. The positive deterministic steps satisfy $\sum_n\alpha_n=\infty$ and $\sum_n\alpha_n^2<\infty$.
3. $\mathbb E[\eta_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=0$ and $\mathbb E[\eta_{n+1}^2\mid\mathcal F_n]\le\sigma^2<\infty$ uniformly in $n$.

Then $w_n\to w^*$ almost surely. The derivative lower bound makes $g$ strictly increasing and unbounded in both directions, so its root exists and is unique. The upper bound prevents arbitrarily steep drift. The conditional noise assumptions allow dependence on past iterates; independent Gaussian noise is not required.

This is an explicitly sufficient version used in these notes. The uniform variance bound is stronger than merely saying that each individual noise variance is finite. It is the bound used in the proof below and in the textbook's Dvoretzky discussion.

The two series conditions serve different purposes:

- $\sum\alpha_n=\infty$ leaves enough total movement to eliminate an initial error.
- $\sum\alpha_n^2<\infty$ limits the accumulated squared noise contribution.

For $\alpha_n=c/(n+1)^p$, both hold when $1/2<p\le1$. They do not guarantee convergence without conditions on drift and noise. A biased observation, unstable drift, or changing target can invalidate the conclusion.

## A Dvoretzky-style error recursion

The source chapter uses Dvoretzky's theorem to connect mean estimation and RM. We can see the mechanism through the following sufficient form:

$$
\Delta_{n+1}=(1-c_n)\Delta_n+d_n\eta_{n+1}.
$$

Assume $c_n$ is determined by $\mathcal F_n$, $0\le c_n\le1$, and $\sum_n c_n=\infty$ almost surely. Let $d_n$ be deterministic with $\sum_n d_n^2<\infty$. Assume $\mathbb E[\Delta_0^2]<\infty$ and the same conditional zero-mean, uniformly bounded-variance noise as above. Then $\Delta_n\to0$ almost surely.

This formulation is narrower than the textbook's general statement: the noise coefficients here are deterministic, and the contraction coefficients are explicitly between zero and one. It is enough for our mean-estimation and RM applications.

### Why this recursion converges

Conditioning on $\mathcal F_n$ eliminates the cross term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[\Delta_{n+1}^2\mid\mathcal F_n]
&=(1-c_n)^2\Delta_n^2+d_n^2\mathbb E[\eta_{n+1}^2\mid\mathcal F_n]\\
&\le\Delta_n^2-c_n\Delta_n^2+d_n^2\sigma^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Define the remaining noise budget $b_n=\sigma^2\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}d_j^2$ and $Z_n=\Delta_n^2+b_n$. Since $b_n-b_{n+1}=\sigma^2d_n^2$,

$$
\mathbb E[Z_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]\le Z_n-c_n\Delta_n^2.
$$

Thus $Z_n$ is a nonnegative supermartingale: its expected next value, conditional on the past, is no larger than its present value. The nonnegative-supermartingale convergence theorem says that such an integrable sequence converges almost surely to a finite limit. This is the probability result used here; the source's Appendix C develops related stochastic convergence tools.

Taking unconditional expectations and summing the inequality gives

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb E[c_n\Delta_n^2]\le\mathbb E[Z_0]<\infty.
$$

Hence $\sum_n c_n\Delta_n^2<\infty$ almost surely. Also, $b_n\to0$, so $\Delta_n^2$ has a limit. If that limit were positive, the divergent sum $\sum_n c_n$ would force $\sum_n c_n\Delta_n^2=\infty$, a contradiction. The limit must be zero.

This proof explains both requirements: contraction must keep acting, and the remaining noise budget must vanish. It also distinguishes almost-sure convergence from a plot that merely looks stable.

### Applying the result to RM

Let $e_n=w_n-w^*$. The mean-value theorem gives $g(w_n)=h_ne_n$ for some $h_n\in[m,M]$; define the slope by the difference quotient when $e_n\ne0$ and choose any allowed slope when it is zero. Then

$$
e_{n+1}=(1-\alpha_nh_n)e_n-\alpha_n\eta_{n+1}.
$$

The coefficient $h_n$ is determined by the current iterate. Since $\alpha_n\to0$, after a finite deterministic prefix we have $0\le\alpha_nh_n\le1$. Moreover, $\sum\alpha_nh_n\ge m\sum\alpha_n=\infty$, and $\sum\alpha_n^2<\infty$. Apply the recursion result with $c_n=\alpha_nh_n$, $d_n=\alpha_n$, and noise $-\eta_{n+1}$. This proves the sufficient RM statement above.

## SGD is stochastic approximation for a gradient equation

Consider

$$
J(w)=\mathbb E[f(w,X)].
$$

When differentiation and expectation can be interchanged—for example, under suitable smoothness and integrable domination—$\nabla J(w)=\mathbb E[\nabla_w f(w,X)]$. A fresh sample gives stochastic gradient descent:

$$
w_{n+1}=w_n-\alpha_n\nabla_w f(w_n,X_{n+1}).
$$

Writing $g(w)=\nabla J(w)$ makes this an RM-type root-finding update. The stochastic gradient must be conditionally unbiased at the current iterate, or satisfy a different theory that handles its bias. Reusing correlated data does not automatically meet the fresh-sample assumption.

For the scalar quadratic loss

$$
f(w,X)=\frac12(w-X)^2,
$$

we have $J'(w)=w-\mu$, so the optimum is $w^*=\mu$. SGD is exactly $w_{n+1}=w_n+\alpha_n(X_{n+1}-w_n)$. Mean estimation, RM, and SGD are three views of the same update in this example.

### Batch, stochastic, and mini-batch gradients

For a fixed dataset of size $N$, the empirical objective is $J_N(w)=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N f(w,x_i)$.

| Method | Gradient used per update | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full-batch GD | Average over all $N$ examples | Exact gradient of $J_N$; relatively expensive per update |
| SGD | One uniformly sampled example | Noisy unbiased gradient of $J_N$ under this sampling rule |
| Mini-batch GD | Average of $B$ independently sampled examples | Under independent sampling, gradient-noise covariance is reduced by a factor of $B$ |

The empirical optimum need not equal the population optimum. Sampling without replacement or using temporally correlated data changes the covariance calculation.

A standard sufficient setting for vector SGD is a smooth strongly convex objective, unbiased gradients with uniformly bounded conditional noise variance, and the two step-size series conditions. A squared-distance bound then has the form

$$
\mathbb E[\|e_{n+1}\|^2\mid\mathcal F_n]
\le(1-2m\alpha_n+L^2\alpha_n^2)\|e_n\|^2+\alpha_n^2\sigma^2,
$$

where $m$ is the strong-convexity constant and $L$ the gradient Lipschitz constant. The same contraction-versus-noise reasoning applies. This does not promise a global optimum for a general nonconvex neural network.

## Step-size experiment using our Gridworld returns

Keep the uniform policy fixed and start every episode at $A$. After the first move, every step succeeds with probability $1/2$. If $K$ counts the number of steps from $B$ or $C$ until entry to $T$, then

$$
\Pr(K=k)=2^{-k},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,\qquad G_0=0.9^K.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mu=\mathbb E[G_0]=\frac{0.45}{1-0.45}=\frac9{11},\qquad
\sigma^2=\frac{0.405}{1-0.405}-\left(\frac9{11}\right)^2
\approx0.0112507813.
$$

This gives both an exact target and an exact return sampler. The script uses NumPy's geometric sampler here; it has the same return distribution as the step-by-step uniform-policy simulator used in Chapter 5.

Run the [script](/rl/ch1-6.py) with optional plotting support:

```bash
python ch1-6.py --output results.json --plot stochastic-approximation.png
```

The plot requires Matplotlib. The experiment uses 200 seeds, numbered 1000–1199, 5,000 samples per seed, and $w_0=0$. All schedules within a seed see the same returns. The recorded environment uses NumPy 1.26.4 and Matplotlib 3.6.3.

### Predicting the three outcomes

For $\alpha_n=1/n$, the theoretical RMSE is $\sigma/\sqrt n$, approximately $0.001500$ at $n=5000$.

For a fixed $0<\alpha<1$ and independent observations,

$$
\mathbb E[e_n]=(1-\alpha)^ne_0,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(e_n)\longrightarrow\frac{\alpha\sigma^2}{2-\alpha}.
$$

The mean bias disappears, but the limiting variance is nonzero. With $\alpha=0.05$, the asymptotic RMSE is approximately $0.016985$. Constant steps can be useful for tracking a drifting target precisely because they keep giving recent samples weight.

For $\alpha_n=1/(n+1)^2$,

$$
\prod_{n=1}^N\left(1-\frac1{(n+1)^2}\right)=\frac{N+2}{2(N+1)}\longrightarrow\frac12.
$$

Thus $\mathbb E[e_N]$ retains half the initial error. Starting at zero, the expected limiting estimate is $\mu/2=0.409091$. This is a direct counterexample to the claim that any step size tending to zero will find the target.

## What this prepares us for

The MC update has the form

$$
\text{estimate}\leftarrow\text{estimate}
+\text{step size}\times(\text{sampled target}-\text{estimate}).
$$

Temporal-difference learning will use a target containing another current value estimate, such as $R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})$. Its noise and coupling require new analysis; the scalar independent-mean theorem should not simply be copied over. [Chapter 7](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-7-时序差分方法) develops those targets and their tabular assumptions.

## Check your understanding

1. Is conditional zero-mean noise the same as unconditional zero mean? **No:** dependence on the current iterate can make the conditional mean nonzero even if the overall mean vanishes.
2. Does $\alpha_n\to0$ imply convergence to the desired root? **No:** the summable $1/(n+1)^2$ example retains bias.
3. Why does the sample-mean update use a per-pair count in MC? **Each pair has its own sample sequence**, and therefore its own averaging denominator.
4. Does fixed-step SGD necessarily stop moving around a stationary optimum? **No:** persistent observation noise generally creates a nonzero fluctuation scale.
