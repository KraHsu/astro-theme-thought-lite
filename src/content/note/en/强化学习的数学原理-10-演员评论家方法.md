**Question for this chapter:** how can a policy improve before complete Monte Carlo returns are available? An **actor** represents the policy; a **critic** estimates the values needed for the actor's update. [Chapter 9](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-9-策略梯度方法) supplies the policy gradient, while [Chapters 7](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-7-时序差分方法) and [8](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-8-价值函数近似) supply value learning.

[Series guide](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-0-阅读指南) · [中文摘要](/note/强化学习的数学原理-10-演员评论家方法)

We will derive Q-based and advantage-based updates, separate action correction from state-distribution correction off-policy, and use a continuous-action example to check deterministic policy gradients. The main objective stays $J(\theta)=\rho^Tv_{\pi_\theta}$, with fixed start $A$ and $\gamma=0.9$ in the grid.

## The simplest Q actor–critic

Let $\pi_\theta$ be the actor and $Q_w$ an action-value critic. A sampled policy-gradient term is

$$
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)Q_w(S_t,A_t).
$$

If $Q_w=q_{\pi_\theta}$, this is the correct conditional action contribution. To obtain the fixed-start discounted gradient, it must still be weighted by the appropriate occupancy. For a trajectory starting from $\rho$, use the per-episode sum of $\gamma^t$-weighted contributions, as in Chapter 9.

The critic can use a Sarsa target:

$$
\delta_t^Q=R_{t+1}+\gamma Q_w(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})-Q_w(S_t,A_t),
\qquad
w\leftarrow w+\alpha_w\delta_t^Q\nabla_wQ_w(S_t,A_t).
$$

The next action follows the current actor; terminal bootstraps are zero. The actor ascends and the critic moves its prediction toward a target. They are optimizing different quantities. A small critic regression loss does not directly measure policy performance.

For a tabular Q critic, only the observed pair changes. With shared parameters, use the semi-gradient convention from Chapter 8. During the actor update, treat the critic output as a fixed numerical estimate; differentiating it through $w$ or through its policy dependence would add terms not present in this policy-gradient estimator.

Basic Q actor–critic, or QAC, replaces the MC return in REINFORCE with a learned $Q$. That permits transition-level value updates but introduces critic approximation error. In the experiment the actor remains frozen for an episode, its weighted gradient terms accumulate, and one actor update occurs afterward. This makes comparison with the fixed-parameter policy-gradient derivation explicit. The critic learns during that episode.

## Advantage actor–critic and the TD error

Chapter 9 proved that an action-independent baseline does not change the expected policy gradient. Choosing $b(s)=v_\pi(s)$ gives the advantage

$$
A_\pi(s,a)=q_\pi(s,a)-v_\pi(s).
$$

A state-value critic $V_w$ supplies the TD error

$$
\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V_w(S_{t+1})-V_w(S_t).
$$

If the critic is exact and the environment sample is correct,

$$
\mathbb E[\delta_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a]
=\bar r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v_\pi(s')-v_\pi(s)
=A_\pi(s,a).
$$

Thus a sampled TD error can replace an advantage estimate. A basic A2C update uses

$$
\begin{aligned}
w&\leftarrow w+\alpha_w\delta_t\nabla_w V_w(S_t),\\
\theta&\leftarrow\theta+\alpha_\theta\gamma^t
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\delta_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

These formulas describe the local increments for our episode objective. Accumulating actor increments with a frozen actor gives the implementation used below. Continuously changing the actor at every transition is also common, but then the fixed-policy expectation argument becomes a local approximation. “A2C” here means the basic advantage actor–critic update; no parallel-worker training system is implied.

### The bias from an imperfect critic

Write $V_w=v_\pi+e$. Then

$$
\mathbb E[\delta_t\mid s,a]
=A_\pi(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)e(s')-e(s).
$$

The final $-e(s)$ is action-independent and cancels in the actor's conditional mean score. The next-state error need not cancel because actions influence where the agent goes. This is why an approximate TD error is not automatically an unbiased advantage sample.

For a concrete grid transition, take the exact uniform-policy critic. At $B$, choosing down yields

$$
\delta=1-v(B)=1/11;
$$

choosing right loops at $B$ and yields

$$
\delta=0.9v(B)-v(B)=-1/11.
$$

The corresponding logistic scores are $-1/2$ for down and $1/2$ for right. Both products are $-1/22$, pushing the right-action logit downward. At $A$, either action has one-step TD error zero under the exact symmetric critic; the policy there has no strict preference.

### Why two learning rates?

The critic evaluates a policy that changes as the actor learns. A common theoretical construction uses two time scales: critic steps $\alpha_k$ and actor steps $\beta_k$, both satisfying suitable stochastic-approximation sums, with $\beta_k/\alpha_k\to0$. The critic then tracks an approximately frozen actor. Stable critic dynamics, exploration, regularity, and boundedness assumptions are still required; this rate ratio alone proves nothing about a neural actor–critic.

Our finite experiments use constant steps and do not claim that asymptotic theorem. A learned baseline in REINFORCE and a bootstrapped critic in A2C also have different bias properties: an action-independent baseline can preserve the mean even when inaccurate, whereas bootstrapping from inaccurate next-state values can bias the actor.

## Off-policy learning: what does an importance ratio correct?

Suppose behavior $\beta(a\mid s)$ generates actions while $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ is the target. At a fixed state, define

$$
\omega(s,a)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\beta(a\mid s)}.
$$

If $\beta(a\mid s)>0$ whenever the target has positive probability, then

$$
\mathbb E_{A\sim\beta}[\omega(s,A)f(s,A)]
=\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)f(s,a).
$$

This identity follows by cancellation of $\beta$. It corrects **actions at the given state**. It does not change the frequency with which that state appears in the dataset.

For example, with target probabilities $(0.8,0.2)$, behavior $(0.5,0.5)$, and action values $(1,3)$, the unweighted mean is 2. The weighted mean is $0.5(1.6)(1)+0.5(0.4)(3)=1.4$, which equals the target mean. If behavior never samples the second action, no finite ratio can recover its contribution.

### State weighting for the actor

Let $m_{\rho,\pi}$ be the unnormalized target occupancy derived in Chapter 9. If transitions are sampled with state distribution $d_b$, then the exact actor gradient, using an exact critic, is

$$
\nabla J=
\mathbb E_{S\sim d_b,A\sim\beta}
\left[
\frac{m_{\rho,\pi}(S)}{d_b(S)}
\omega(S,A)\nabla\log\pi_\theta(A\mid S)A_\pi(S,A)
\right].
$$

Both state and action support are required. Here the state factor uses an unnormalized occupancy. If both occupancies are normalized, the separate factor $1/(1-\gamma)$ must appear.

Even changing the objective to $J_b=\sum_s d_b(s)v_\pi(s)$ with fixed $d_b$ does not generally justify using $d_b$ directly as the actor's gradient weighting. Differentiating that objective produces

$$
m_b^T=d_b^T(I-\gamma P_\pi)^{-1},
$$

followed by the same local policy derivative. This is the weighting appearing in the source's off-policy theorem. It is distinct from the raw behavior-state distribution and is generally not normalized. Calling both quantities “the state distribution” hides an important distinction.

### State correction without an explicit model

For a whole trajectory generated by $\beta$ from the same start distribution, define the prefix product

$$
W_{0:t}=\prod_{k=0}^{t}\frac{\pi_\theta(A_k\mid S_k)}{\beta(A_k\mid S_k)}.
$$

It changes the probability of the trajectory prefix through the current action from behavior to target. With a frozen target policy and an exact target critic,

$$
\mathbb E_\beta\left[
\sum_t\gamma^t W_{0:t}
\nabla\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\delta_t
\right]=\nabla J.
$$

The next transition requires no extra action ratio because its environment law is already conditional on $(S_t,A_t)$. Prefix products implicitly correct state visitation as well as the current action. They can have very high variance; clipping them trades exactness for a different, biased estimator. This is a mathematically explicit alternative to pretending the state weighting is automatically correct.

### The critic needs its own correction

For a state-value critic updated from behavior samples, a tabular off-policy TD increment is

$$
V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha_w\omega_t
[R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})-V(S_t)].
$$

At a fixed visited state its conditional mean is $T_\pi V-V$. The critic needs the current action ratio to evaluate the target rather than behavior. The actor additionally needs the correct state weighting for its stated objective. Adequate visits and appropriate steps matter even tabularly; ordinary off-policy semi-gradient TD with shared features can diverge, as Chapter 8 demonstrated.

A basic episodic off-policy actor–critic can therefore freeze the target, gather a behavior episode, update the tabular critic using $\omega_t\delta_t$, and accumulate actor terms with $\gamma^tW_{0:t}\delta_t$. Exact-critic expectations justify its actor direction; with a learned critic it remains approximate. We describe this algorithm and verify the weighting identity below, rather than claim that prefix-weighted learning will be low-variance.

## Deterministic actor–critic

For continuous actions, a deterministic actor outputs $a=\mu_\theta(s)$. There is no ordinary discrete-action log-probability score to use. Instead, differentiate the critic with respect to its action input.

For finite states with smooth continuous-action rewards and transition probabilities, define

$$
q_\mu(s,a)=\bar r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v_\mu(s').
$$

Differentiating $v_\mu(s)=q_\mu(s,\mu_\theta(s))$ separates the immediate action derivative from the effect on future policy decisions:

$$
\nabla_\theta v_\mu(s)=
\nabla_\theta\mu_\theta(s)\,
\left.\nabla_aq_\mu(s,a)\right|_{a=\mu_\theta(s)}
+\gamma\sum_{s'}P_\mu(s,s')\nabla_\theta v_\mu(s').
$$

In the action derivative, the future policy is held fixed. Solving the same Bellman derivative recursion gives

$$
\nabla_\theta J=
\sum_s m_{\rho,\mu}(s)
\nabla_\theta\mu_\theta(s)\,
\left.\nabla_aq_\mu(s,a)\right|_{a=\mu_\theta(s)}.
$$

The Jacobian product has the parameter-gradient orientation; in column-Jacobian notation transpose the actor Jacobian accordingly. Smoothness and differentiation-under-expectation conditions are needed. This theorem is not a derivative of an argmax over the grid's two discrete actions.

A practical critic target for replay transition $(s,a,r,s')$ is

$$
y=r+\gamma(1-z)Q_{\bar w}(s',\mu_{\bar\theta}(s')),
$$

where $z$ marks termination and bars denote optional slowly updated target parameters. Regress $Q_w(s,a)$ toward $y$, then update the actor using $\nabla_\theta\mu_\theta(s)\nabla_aQ_w(s,a)|_{a=\mu_\theta(s)}$. The behavior can add exploration noise or use another policy, while the bootstrap action comes from the deterministic target actor.

No action likelihood ratio is needed for this one-step Q regression: conditional on $(s,a)$, the observed reward and next state are valid samples. However, actor gradients averaged over arbitrary replay states are not automatically the exact fixed-start gradient; the target occupancy still matters. Coverage near the actor's actions also matters for the accuracy of the critic's action derivative. Target networks and replay do not supply a global convergence guarantee.

### A continuous-action example with a known answer

Use a separate one-step task: one initial state, action $a\in[-1,1]$, reward

$$
R=-(a-0.7)^2+\xi,\qquad \mathbb E[\xi\mid a]=0,
$$

and immediate termination. Then $J(\theta)=-(\theta-0.7)^2$ for $\mu_\theta=\theta$, so

$$
\frac{dJ}{d\theta}=2(0.7-\theta),\qquad \theta^*=0.7.
$$

At $\theta=0.2$, the derivative is 1; an ascent step of size 0.01 moves to 0.21. A quadratic critic $Q_w(a)=w_0+w_1a+w_2a^2$ can represent the exact mean reward with coefficients $(-0.49,1.4,-1)$. Its actor derivative is $w_1+2w_2\theta$.

The script samples behavior actions uniformly on $[-1,1]$, adds Gaussian reward noise with standard deviation 0.01, fits the critic by SGD with step 0.05, and starts actor updates after 200 critic samples. Actor step is 0.01; projection to $[-1,1]$ enforces the action constraint. There is only one decision state, so this example has no state-distribution mismatch. It isolates critic fitting and the action chain rule; it is not a test of long-horizon DDPG stability.

## What the ten chapters now connect

The path is continuous: MDP modeling defines the return; Bellman equations evaluate policies; optimality equations support planning; MC replaces model expectations with full returns; stochastic approximation explains noisy updates; TD bootstraps; function approximation shares parameters; policy gradients optimize action probabilities; actor–critic uses learned values inside those gradients.

Each transition adds assumptions rather than erasing earlier ones. A fixed point, a good finite curve, an unbiased gradient, and a convergent control algorithm are distinct claims. PPO, SAC, and larger continuous-control systems are useful next topics, but they require their own objectives, implementation details, and evidence.

## Check your understanding

1. When is a TD error an unbiased advantage sample? **When the state-value critic equals the target policy's true value and the transition sample is correct**, conditioning on the current state and action.
2. Does baseline invariance remove all critic bias in A2C? **No.** An action-dependent next-state error survives the score expectation.
3. Does $\pi/\beta$ correct a behavior-state distribution? **No.** It corrects the conditional action distribution; occupancy or trajectory-prefix correction addresses states.
4. Why can deterministic Q learning use replay actions different from the actor's action? **The critic is trained at the recorded $(s,a)$ and bootstraps using the target actor at $s'$.** Its action derivative still requires relevant data coverage.
5. What does the one-step continuous experiment prove? **It checks the action chain rule and learns a critic with a known optimum.** It says little about long-horizon nonlinear actor–critic stability.
