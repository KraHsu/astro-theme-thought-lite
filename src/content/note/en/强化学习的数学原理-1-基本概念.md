This revision uses one small environment throughout the ten chapters so that the definitions can be checked with numbers; separate counterexamples and the continuous-action task are identified explicitly.

**Question for this chapter:** what exactly must we specify before an agent can learn? By the end, we should be able to describe an MDP, generate a trajectory, and calculate its return. Probability distributions and geometric series are the only prerequisites here.

## One step of interaction

At time $t$, the agent is in state $S_t$, chooses action $A_t$, and then receives reward $R_{t+1}$ and next state $S_{t+1}$:

$$
S_t \xrightarrow{A_t} (R_{t+1},S_{t+1}).
$$

Capital letters denote random variables; lowercase letters denote their particular values. The action belongs to time $t$, while the resulting reward belongs to time $t+1$. Keeping this convention avoids many indexing mistakes later.

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $\mathcal S$ | Set of states |
| $\mathcal A(s)$ | Actions available at state $s$ |
| $\pi(a\mid s)$ | Probability that the agent chooses $a$ in $s$ |
| $p(s',r\mid s,a)$ | Joint distribution of the next state and reward |
| $p(s'\mid s,a)$ | State-transition probability |
| $\bar r(s,a)$ | Expected immediate reward |
| $\gamma$ | Discount factor |
| $G_t$ | Discounted return starting at time $t$ |

A **stationary policy** uses the same conditional distribution at every time:

$$
\pi(a\mid s)\ge0,\qquad \sum_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}\pi(a\mid s)=1.
$$

A deterministic policy assigns probability one to one action. A stochastic policy may assign positive probability to several actions. Different states may have different action sets, although the example below uses the same two actions everywhere.

## The environment model and the Markov property

Let $H_t=(S_0,A_0,R_1,S_1,\ldots,A_{t-1},R_t,S_t)$ be the observed interaction history. The Markov assumption says that, after conditioning on the current state and action, the earlier history adds no information about the next outcome:

$$
\Pr(S_{t+1}=s',R_{t+1}=r\mid H_t,A_t=a)
=p(s',r\mid S_t,a).
$$

Consequently, the state-transition and reward marginals obey

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(S_{t+1}=s'\mid H_t,A_t=a)&=p(s'\mid S_t,a),\\
\Pr(R_{t+1}=r\mid H_t,A_t=a)&=p(r\mid S_t,a).
\end{aligned}
$$

For a discrete model,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(s'\mid s,a)&=\sum_r p(s',r\mid s,a),\\
p(r\mid s,a)&=\sum_{s'}p(s',r\mid s,a),\\
\bar r(s,a)&=\sum_{s',r}r\,p(s',r\mid s,a).
\end{aligned}
$$

Every joint probability is nonnegative and $\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)=1$. The two marginals need not be independent. For example, if entering a goal produces a reward, the next state and reward are directly related.

A **reward** is a realized number; a **reward distribution** describes uncertainty about that number; an **expected reward** averages over this uncertainty. Positive and negative rewards influence what the agent prefers, but their meaning comes from the return objective, not from a universal definition of “good” and “bad.”

### An observation is not automatically a state

A robot might observe only its current position. Two visits to that position with different velocities can have different next-position distributions under the same force. Position alone is then not a Markov state. Adding velocity may make the model Markov under the assumed dynamics.

The Markov property is a modeling claim about sufficient information. It does not mean that the world has no history, or that an arbitrary sensor reading contains everything needed for prediction.

## A Gridworld we can calculate by hand

The following is a deliberately small example for these notes, rather than a reproduction of a textbook figure:

| | Left column | Right column |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Top row | $A$ — start | $B$ |
| Bottom row | $C$ | $T$ — terminal |

There are two actions: right ($R$) and down ($D$). A move beyond the grid boundary leaves the state unchanged. Entering $T$ yields reward $1$; every other transition yields $0$. Once at $T$, both actions keep the agent there with reward $0$. The transitions are deterministic and $\gamma=0.9$.

| State | Action $R$: (next state, reward) | Action $D$: (next state, reward) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ | $(B,0)$ | $(C,0)$ |
| $B$ | $(B,0)$ | $(T,1)$ |
| $C$ | $(T,1)$ | $(C,0)$ |
| $T$ | $(T,0)$ | $(T,0)$ |

The **uniform policy** chooses each action with probability $1/2$. Although the environment is deterministic, trajectories under this policy are random. A model can also have stochastic transitions even when its policy is deterministic.

The complete table lets us write any probability we need. For example,

$$
p(T,1\mid B,D)=1,\quad p(B,0\mid B,R)=1,\quad \bar r(B,D)=1.
$$

## Rewards, returns, and episodes

A trajectory records states, actions, and rewards in their temporal order. One possible episode under the uniform policy is

$$
A\xrightarrow[R_1=0]{R}B
\xrightarrow[R_2=0]{R}B
\xrightarrow[R_3=1]{D}T.
$$

The discounted return is

$$
G_t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^kR_{t+k+1}
=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}.
$$

For this episode, $G_0=0+0.9\cdot0+0.9^2\cdot1=0.81$. Taking $R$ and then $D$ would give $G_0=0.9$. The final reward is identical, but delaying it reduces its discounted contribution.

In the discounted infinite-horizon setting, assume $0\le\gamma<1$ and bounded rewards $|R_t|\le R_{\max}$. Then

$$
|G_t|\le\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma},
$$

so the return is well-defined even if an episode never terminates. A discount factor of zero values only the next reward. The undiscounted case $\gamma=1$ needs separate assumptions, such as suitable termination and integrability; the discounted proofs in the next chapters do not cover it automatically.

An **episode** ends when a terminal state is reached. A **continuing task** has no such interaction endpoint. To represent an episodic task with infinite-horizon notation, append an absorbing terminal state with zero rewards after termination. This preserves the original return.

Allowing the agent to leave the goal and repeatedly collect reward changes the task. That construction is not generally equivalent to the original episodic problem. Likewise, a rollout time limit is not automatically a genuine terminal state: truncating a continuing trajectory omits its future return.

## The MDP and the learning problem

We can now describe a finite discounted MDP by

$$
\mathcal M=(\mathcal S,\{\mathcal A(s)\}_{s\in\mathcal S},p,\gamma),
$$

where $p(s',r\mid s,a)$ includes both transition and reward information. Another common convention separates the transition model and reward model into different tuple entries. These are notation choices; what matters is specifying the same information.

For Chapters 1–4, assume finite state/action sets, bounded rewards, a time-homogeneous model, and stationary Markov policies. An initial-state distribution tells us where episodes begin; our experiment starts at $A$ unless it explicitly uses exploring starts.

There are two different questions:

- **Prediction / policy evaluation:** how much return does a fixed policy achieve?
- **Control:** which policy achieves the greatest expected return?

An MDP does not imply that the agent knows its model. Chapters 2–4 calculate with a known model. Chapter 5 uses sampled episodes instead of transition probabilities; Chapter 6 studies why noisy incremental updates can converge.

## Check your understanding

1. At $B$, what are the next-state probabilities under the uniform policy? **Answer:** $p_\pi(B\mid B)=p_\pi(T\mid B)=1/2$.
2. What is the return from $A$ if the reward arrives on the fourth transition? **Answer:** $0.9^3=0.729$.
3. Can a deterministic environment generate random returns? **Answer:** yes, through a stochastic policy or random initial conditions.
4. Why is the value of $T$ zero in this example? **Answer:** the reward was obtained when entering $T$; all subsequent rewards are zero.

The central distinction is now precise: the model describes the environment, the policy describes the agent, and the return describes the objective.
