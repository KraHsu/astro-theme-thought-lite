**Question for this chapter:** why does an optimal policy exist, and how does the Bellman optimality equation identify it? We will answer four questions: existence, uniqueness, deterministic versus stochastic policies, and computation. We use the finite discounted MDP assumptions and notation from Chapters 1–2.

[Series guide](/en/note/强化学习的数学原理-0-阅读指南) · [中文摘要](/note/强化学习的数学原理-3-贝尔曼最优化公式)

## From evaluating a policy to choosing one

Say that $\pi_1$ is at least as good as $\pi_2$ when

$$
v_{\pi_1}(s)\ge v_{\pi_2}(s),\qquad\forall s\in\mathcal S.
$$

This is a partial ordering: two policies can each be better in different states. An optimal policy $\pi^*$ must satisfy $v_{\pi^*}(s)\ge v_\pi(s)$ for every state and every competing policy, rather than only for a chosen starting state.

For an arbitrary vector $v$, define a one-step lookahead quantity

$$
q_v(s,a)=\bar r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v(s').
$$

It is not necessarily the action-value function of any policy. It becomes $q_\pi$ when $v=v_\pi$, and $q^*$ when $v=v^*$.

Two operators make the difference between prediction and control explicit:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(T_\pi v)(s)&=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)q_v(s,a),\\
(T_*v)(s)&=\max_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}q_v(s,a).
\end{aligned}
$$

The **Bellman optimality equation** is $v=T_*v$. Equivalently,

$$
v(s)=\max_a\left[\bar r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v(s')\right].
$$

It can also be written $v=\max_\pi(r_\pi+\gamma P_\pi v)$, with an elementwise maximum. The matrix definition remains $[P_\pi]_{s,s'}=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)p(s'\mid s,a)$; there is no extra sum over $s'$ inside a single matrix entry.

### Why maximizing over actions is enough

For any probability vector $c$, $\sum_a c_aq_a\le\max_a q_a$, because it is a weighted average. Equality is attained by putting all mass on a maximizing action. Therefore,

$$
\max_{\pi(\cdot\mid s)}\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)q_a=\max_a q_a.
$$

If several actions tie, any distribution supported on those actions attains the same maximum. Write $a^*(s)\in\arg\max_a q_a$ to acknowledge that argmax can be a set.

## A complete fixed-point argument

A map $F:X\to X$ is a contraction if there is $0\le c<1$ such that $d(Fx,Fy)\le c\,d(x,y)$. If the nonempty metric space $X$ is complete, then $F$ has exactly one fixed point, and the iteration $x_{k+1}=F(x_k)$ converges to it from every $x_0\in X$.

Here is the proof, including the step that small successive changes alone do not establish.

For $c<1$, repeated application of the contraction bound gives

$$
d(x_{k+1},x_k)\le c^k d(x_1,x_0).
$$

For any integers $m>n$, the triangle inequality gives

$$
d(x_m,x_n)\le\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}c^kd(x_1,x_0)
\le\frac{c^n}{1-c}d(x_1,x_0)\longrightarrow0.
$$

Thus $(x_k)$ is Cauchy. Completeness provides a limit $x^*\in X$. Since a contraction is continuous, $F(x^*)=\lim_kF(x_k)=\lim_kx_{k+1}=x^*$. If $y^*$ were another fixed point, then

$$
d(x^*,y^*)=d(Fx^*,Fy^*)\le c\,d(x^*,y^*),
$$

forcing $d(x^*,y^*)=0$. Finally, $d(x_k,x^*)\le c^kd(x_0,x^*)$. When $c=0$, $F$ is constant and one update already reaches its fixed point.

For our problem, $X=\mathbb R^{|\mathcal S|}$ with the infinity norm is complete. The norm must be specified: proving contraction in one norm is not the same as proving it in every norm.

## Why the Bellman optimality operator is a contraction

For two finite sets of numbers indexed by the same actions,

$$
\left|\max_a x_a-\max_a y_a\right|\le\max_a|x_a-y_a|.
$$

To see this, choose an action maximizing $x$ and bound $\max x-\max y$ by its difference at that action; then exchange $x$ and $y$ for the reverse inequality.

The reward terms in $q_v-q_w$ cancel. Since transition probabilities are nonnegative and sum to one,

$$
\begin{aligned}
|(T_*v)(s)-(T_*w)(s)|
&\le\max_a\gamma\left|\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)(v(s')-w(s'))\right|\\
&\le\gamma\|v-w\|_\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the maximum over $s$ proves $\|T_*v-T_*w\|_\infty\le\gamma\|v-w\|_\infty$. Thus $T_*$ has a unique fixed point $v^*$, and

$$
v_{k+1}=T_*v_k,\qquad
\|v_k-v^*\|_\infty\le\gamma^k\|v_0-v^*\|_\infty.
$$

This proves existence, uniqueness, and an algorithm for the fixed point. We still need to prove that the fixed point represents optimal performance.

## Why the fixed point is optimal

For any stationary policy $\pi$, maximizing over actions gives

$$
d_\pi:=v^*-T_\pi v^*\ge0.
$$

Subtracting $v_\pi=T_\pi v_\pi$ yields

$$
(I-\gamma P_\pi)(v^*-v_\pi)=d_\pi.
$$

The inverse has nonnegative entries by the Neumann series in Chapter 2. Therefore

$$
v^*-v_\pi=(I-\gamma P_\pi)^{-1}d_\pi\ge0.
$$

Now choose a greedy policy $\pi^*$ supported on maximizers of $q_{v^*}$. It satisfies $T_{\pi^*}v^*=T_*v^*=v^*$. Policy evaluation has a unique solution, so $v_{\pi^*}=v^*$. The upper bound is attainable.

Even a history-dependent policy cannot beat it: $v^*(s)\ge\mathbb E[R_{t+1}+\gamma v^*(S_{t+1})\mid H_t,S_t=s]$ for any action distribution. Iterating this inequality bounds the expected first $n$ discounted rewards plus $\gamma^nv^*(S_n)$ by $v^*(S_0)$. The last term vanishes because $v^*$ is bounded and $\gamma<1$. Hence the full expected return is at most $v^*$.

This answers the four opening questions:

| Question | Answer under our finite discounted assumptions |
| --- | --- |
| Does an optimal policy exist? | Yes; a policy greedy with respect to $v^*$ attains it. |
| Is it unique? | The value vector is unique; the policy need not be. |
| Must it be deterministic? | A deterministic stationary optimum exists; ties can also allow stochastic optima. |
| How can we obtain it? | Solve $v=T_*v$, then select maximizing actions. Chapter 4 develops the algorithms. |

## Optimal values in our Gridworld

The shortest route reaches $T$ in two transitions from $A$ and one from $B$ or $C$. Thus

$$
v^*=(0.9,1,1,0)^T.
$$

Substitution into $T_*$ confirms the result. The optimal action values are

| State | $q^*(s,R)$ | $q^*(s,D)$ | Optimal actions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ | $0.9$ | $0.9$ | Either or any mixture |
| $B$ | $0.9$ | $1$ | $D$ |
| $C$ | $1$ | $0.9$ | $R$ |
| $T$ | $0$ | $0$ | Either |

At $A$, both actions are optimal. This is a concrete example of a unique $v^*$ with multiple optimal policies. The [experiment script](/rl/ch1-6.py) independently enumerates all eight deterministic policies on the three nonterminal states to verify these values.

## Reward transformations and their boundaries

For an infinite discounted reward sequence, replace **every** reward by $r'=ar+b$, where $a>0$. For each trajectory,

$$
G'_t=aG_t+\frac{b}{1-\gamma}.
$$

Consequently, $v'_\pi=av_\pi+b\mathbf1/(1-\gamma)$ for every policy. Positive scaling preserves inequalities, so the optimal policies are unchanged and $v'^*=av^*+b\mathbf1/(1-\gamma)$.

For example, $a=2$, $b=0.3$, and $\gamma=0.9$ transform the optimal vector into $(4.8,5,5,3)^T$. Notice that the absorbing state's value becomes $3$: its formerly zero rewards must also become $0.3$ on every subsequent step for this formula to apply.

Two failures are worth calculating:

- **Negative scaling:** in a one-state continuing task with rewards $1$ and $2$, multiplying rewards by $-1$ changes the preferred action from the second to the first. The requirement is $a>0$, not merely $a\ne0$.
- **Adding a constant only until termination:** compare a one-step episode with reward $1$ to a two-step episode with rewards $(0,1)$ at $\gamma=0.9$. Their returns are $1$ and $0.9$. Adding $1$ to each actual pre-termination reward gives $2$ and $1+0.9\cdot2=2.8$, reversing the preference. The added amount depends on episode duration.

Changing the discount factor can also change optimal actions. An immediate reward of $1$ beats a reward of $2$ delayed by one step when $\gamma<1/2$, while the delayed option wins when $\gamma>1/2$. These alternatives define a separate small decision problem, not a changed rule hidden inside our Gridworld.

## Check your understanding

1. Why is showing a fixed point insufficient? **Answer:** one must also show that it dominates all policies and is attained by some policy.
2. Can a stochastic policy at $B$ be optimal here? **Answer:** only if it assigns probability one to $D$; putting positive mass on $R$ loses value.
3. What is wrong with “the iterates get closer, therefore they converge”? **Answer:** successive differences tending to zero do not imply the Cauchy property; the summable geometric bound supplies the missing argument.
